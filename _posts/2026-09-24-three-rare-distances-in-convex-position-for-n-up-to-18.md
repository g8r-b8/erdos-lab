## Background

[Erdős Problem #132](https://www.erdosproblems.com/132) asks whether every set of $$n$$ points in the plane has
two distances that each occur at least once and at most $$n$$ times. Call such a distance *rare*. The diameter
is always rare (Hopf–Pannwitz), so the question is about a second one.

For points in convex position, Clemen, Dumitrescu and Liu ([arXiv:2505.04283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04283),
Theorem 1.2) proved that the answer is yes: there are at least two rare distances. For $$n = 5$$ this is best
possible, since the regular pentagon has only two distances. A natural next question is whether convex sets
must have *more* rare distances as $$n$$ grows.

For small $$n$$ the classical literature already says something. Erdős and Fishburn (1995) record that in a
convex $$n$$-gon with $$n \le 8$$ the second-largest multiplicity is at most $$n$$. Together with Altman's
lower bound on the number of distinct distances, this gives three rare distances for $$n = 6, 7, 8$$. We found
nothing beyond that.

## The result

**Theorem.** Every set of $$n$$ points in strictly convex position with $$6 \le n \le 18$$ has at least three
rare distances. The same holds for every even $$n \ge 6$$.

This is a partial result on the convex case of #132, not a solution to the problem. The cases have different
status:

- **$$n = 6, 7, 8$$:** a corollary of published results (Altman; Erdős–Fishburn 1995).
- **Even $$n \ge 10$$:** CDL's own argument, with the counting done with attention to parity. This is a small
  sharpening of their proof.
- **$$n = 9$$:** from Erdős and Fishburn's 1996 classification of convex nonagons with five distances, plus
  counting.
- **$$n = 11, 13, 15, 17$$:** new, computer-assisted.

For $$n = 11$$ and $$13$$ (and $$7$$) the statement is also a Lean 4 theorem about finite point sets:

```lean
theorem erdos132_convex13_finset (S : Finset Pt) (hn : S.card = 13)
    (hS : ConvexIndependent ℝ ((↑) : S → Pt)) : 3 ≤ (rareDistsS S).card
```

## Idea of the proof

**Counting.** Let $$D$$ be the number of distinct distances and $$k$$ the number of rare ones. Frequent
distances use at least $$n+1$$ pairs each, so $$(D-k)(n+1) + k \le \binom{n}{2}$$. If $$k \le 2$$ this forces
$$D \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1$$. Altman showed $$D \ge \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$$ for convex sets, so only a
narrow window of $$D$$ remains. For even $$n$$ the parity of the count closes the window, using Fishburn's
description of the extremal sets. For odd $$n$$ it does not, and that is where the computer comes in.

**An ordinal abstraction.** Label the points in cyclic order and colour each pair by the rank of its distance.
The one geometric fact used is the quadrilateral inequality: in a convex quadrilateral the two diagonals are
together longer than either pair of opposite sides. In terms of ranks, the two diagonal colours cannot both be
at most the colours of a pair of opposite sides. Colourings with this property are finite objects. This is
the method of Morić and Pritchard ([arXiv:1103.0412](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.0412)), who searched a finite
abstract version of a convex distance problem using the same fact; the ordinal use of the inequality goes back
to Altman.

**SAT.** For each odd $$n$$ and each $$D$$ in the window, the statement "there is such a colouring with at most
two rare colour classes" is written as a CNF formula. Every one is unsatisfiable. For $$n = 15, 17$$ the
instances are split by which two colours may be rare, and dihedral symmetry is broken. The solver is never
trusted:

- $$n = 11, 13$$: the solver's proofs were checked by `drat-trim`, and its LRAT output re-checked by
  `lrat-check`. Independently, the whole statement, including the passage from geometry to colourings, is
  proved in Lean 4 with Mathlib. The finite core is closed by Lean's `bv_decide`, which checks an LRAT
  certificate with a checker proved correct in Lean. The trusted base on this route is Lean plus **one extra
  axiom per theorem**, generated by `bv_decide`, asserting that the verified checker returns `true` on the
  concrete certificate. That value comes from running compiled code, so the trust is of the same kind as for
  `native_decide`. Neither our encoder nor any external solver is trusted on this route.
- $$n = 15, 17$$: 54 and 70 cases, each refuted by Kissat and checked by `drat-trim`. The hardest cases took
  up to about 12 minutes and produced proofs of up to about 124 MB. Here the trusted base is the short Python
  encoder, the symmetry-breaking argument, and `drat-trim`. **These cases are not in Lean.**

A pleasant by-product: for every odd $$n$$ from 7 to 17 the quadrilateral inequality *alone* rules out every
configuration with at most two rare distances. No rigidity and no coordinates are needed.

## What is still open

- **Odd $$n \ge 19$$.** The remaining configuration is always the same: $$D = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1$$, with
  all but two distances frequent. The SAT approach looks feasible for $$n = 19$$ but cannot reach every $$n$$;
  a geometric argument is needed.
- Whether the number of rare distances in convex position must tend to infinity with $$n$$.
- Problem #132 itself, for general point sets.
- A human expert has not yet read these proofs. Corrections are welcome in the comments below.
